# Suraj's romantic voice

This skill exists because generic "romantic website copy" reads as AI slop — balanced, symmetrical, safe. ("You + me = us." No.) Suraj already has a voice: five real samples of how he actually writes to Krisha are below. Read them before writing anything, every time this skill is invoked — don't work from the summary alone, the summary flattens exactly the texture that makes this NOT sound generic.

## The core instruction

Write like Suraj wrote these samples, not like a copywriter imitating "a romantic tone." The difference: a copywriter smooths everything into balanced, resolved sentences. Suraj doesn't. He trails off. He drops "because" to "cause." He interrupts a metaphor with a joke, or a joke with something devastatingly sincere. That unevenness IS the voice — do not sand it down.

## Patterns pulled from the samples (use these, don't just read them)

1. **Rhetorical question chains, usually in threes.** "What do you think resilience has cost you the most: your voice, your calm, or your ability to move without second-guessing?" / "What kind of love is it? What kind of intensity do we share? How are we one?" Never one clean question when three half-answered ones hit harder.
2. **Extended metaphor from an unexpected register**, then abandoned before it over-explains. Deserts waiting for rain. Oxytocin and biodiversity next to "hulchul" in the same sentence. A light in the sky he's been chasing, found instead in her eyes. Science vocabulary and nature imagery are both fair game, often in the same breath.
3. **Casual register collides with elevated register, deliberately.** "One heck of a human" next to "life's treasure trove." "ive known you forever" (lowercase, no apostrophe) next to a genuine ache. Don't clean this up into consistent formality — the collision is the personality.
4. **Hindi/Urdu woven in, not footnoted.** Words like "yaps," "hulchul," "atyachaar," full couplets like "uski yaad aayi hai saason zara dheere aahiste chalo, dhadkano se bhi ibaadat mein khalal padta hai." No parenthetical translation mid-flow — if a gloss is needed, it happens in the surrounding English sentence, not as "(translation: ...)".
5. **Hyper-specific, unglamorous concrete objects carry more weight than abstractions.** Not "I miss you" — "the pink sweatshirt has steadily been losing your smell." Not "you're special" — a Tesco club card joke. Ground every abstraction in one real, small, specific thing.
6. **Trailing ellipses for a thought that doesn't resolve.** "...." or " . . ." or "......" — inconsistent dot counts are fine, even correct. This is a voice that trails off mid-feeling on purpose, not a typo to fix.
7. **Negation-then-affirmation.** "You're not a crossing thought in my head... you exist in it, with more grace than I'd like to admit." State what it isn't first, land on what it is.
8. **Self-aware about being poetic — winks at it.** "Its only right on my part to wrap myself in a few metaphors and present it to you ;)" He knows he's being extra. Let a line acknowledge that sometimes, instead of playing it fully straight every time.
9. **Two completely different registers coexist across the body of work: silly/horny-adjacent pickup lines, and devastating sincerity.** Both are equally "him." Don't flatten everything into one tone — match the register to the moment (a pickup-line easter egg is allowed to just be dumb and fun; an anniversary reveal earns the sincere register).
10. **Plain, unmetaphored declarations land hardest placed AFTER a run of imagery, used sparingly.** "I know it in my heart that I love you so much that nothing compares." One plain sentence after three metaphorical ones outweighs four metaphorical ones in a row.
11. **Fragments over complete sentences when a complete sentence would slow it down.** "Intensity diminish and energies diverging." Grammatically loose, emotionally exact.

## Hard rule: no em-dashes as connective tissue

Suraj's own writing barely uses them. Use periods, ellipses, "and," "cause," commas, semicolons at most. An em-dash stitching two clauses together is the single most common tell that a line was generated, not written. If a line needs an em-dash to work, it needs to be rewritten as two sentences instead.

## What this voice never does

- Never a balanced X-plus-Y-equals-Z formula. That's the thing Suraj explicitly rejected ("you plus me equals to us, that is bullshit").
- Never resolves too neatly. A trailing question, a fragment, or an escalating image is a better ending than a tidy bow.
- Never uses "special," "amazing," "perfect," or other inspirational-poster adjectives untethered from a specific image.
- Never stays in one register (all-metaphor or all-plain) for an entire passage. The alternation is the point.
- Never overexplains a joke or a metaphor immediately after landing it. Trust the reader.

## Quoting real poets: Ghalib, Sahir Ludhianvi, Jigar Muradabadi, Kabir

Suraj wants real lines from these poets worked in, in the same natural, unfootnoted way he already weaves in his own Hindi/Urdu couplets — not as a labeled "quote block," just placed where the moment earns it (an anniversary, the puzzle's resolution, a letter). A few he's already effectively quoting the *style* of (the "saanson zara dheere chalo" couplet in his own samples is exactly this register).

**Accuracy is non-negotiable here.** A couplet confidently attributed to Ghalib that Ghalib never wrote is worse than no couplet at all — it's disrespectful to the poet and it's a real risk of embarrassing Suraj in front of Krisha, who may well recognize the real ones. Rules:

- Only use a couplet if you (the model instance actually writing the copy, in that session) are genuinely confident it's real and correctly attributed — not "this sounds like something Ghalib would write." Widely-known, frequently-anthologized lines (e.g. Ghalib's "hazaaron khwahishen aisi," Kabir's "bura jo dekhan main chala") are safer than obscure or Bollywood-recycled ones.
- If you're not confident of an exact couplet, do not fabricate one and attach a poet's name to it. Either write in the spirit of that poet's register without a direct quote, or use a couplet without naming the poet, or skip it.
- One or two placements per experience is plenty. This is seasoning, not the whole dish — the site should still sound like Suraj, occasionally elevated by a line of Ghalib or Kabir, not like an anthology.
- Never translate a couplet inline with a bracketed gloss. If the English context around it doesn't make the feeling legible on its own, the placement is wrong — move it somewhere the surrounding copy already carries the meaning.

## Self-check before shipping any line

1. Read it out loud. Does it sound like a person who is a little embarrassed by how much he feels, trying to be clever about it anyway? Or does it sound like ad copy?
2. Is there an em-dash doing the work two sentences should do?
3. Is every abstraction anchored to one specific, small, real thing?
4. Would this line survive being read back to Krisha out loud without Suraj wincing?
5. If a poet is quoted: are you certain it's real and correctly attributed?
